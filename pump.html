Cell Culture Perfusion Pump.

Leading mechanical design, iterative prototyping, and experimental testing within a five-person senior design team developing a magnetically actuated perfusion pump for six-well cell culture plates.

CAD & Mechanical Assembly3D PrintingDesign IterationFluid TestingData Analysis
01 / Project Overview

Making cell culture perfusion more practical.

Conventional cell culture perfusion setups can be costly and cumbersome, with external tubing that complicates incubator operation and creates potential contamination pathways. Our five-person UC Riverside senior design team developed a compact pump concept integrated with a standard six-well plate and microfluidic insert.

My contributions: I owned the mechanical CAD, physical assembly, iterative design changes, experimental testing, and data analysis. I revised and reprinted components in response to test results, then retested the updated assemblies. The system concept and major actuation decisions were developed collaboratively by the team.

Project scope: Student-built research prototype. The reported flow and pressure measurements are engineering performance results, not evidence of cell viability or validated sterile operation.

02 / Mechanical Engineering

Packaging a closed-loop system.

The pump uses a rotating magnetic field generated beneath the plate to drive a magnet-coupled impeller inside the media reservoir. Fluid circulates through the microfluidic insert and returns to the reservoir, reducing the need for external pumping lines.

I modeled the assembly and its component interfaces, produced 3D-printed parts, and integrated the mechanical elements for prototype testing. The exploded CAD view illustrates how the plate, reservoir, pump components, and supporting structure fit together.

03 / Iterative Development

Design. Print. Test. Refine.

The team initially explored solenoid-coil magnetic actuation, but inconsistent impeller rotation led to a collaborative decision to adopt a fan-driven magnetic approach. I handled the corresponding mechanical implementation.

My iterative process continued beyond that system-level change: after printing and assembling components, I tested performance, investigated mechanical issues, revised the CAD geometry, fabricated updated parts, and retested to evaluate the fixes.

The revised actuation setup used a DC fan and neodymium magnets, with an Arduino-based controller and MOSFET for speed control. These electronics were part of the team's integrated system.

04 / Prototype Validation

Measured performance, visible limitations.

I conducted testing and analyzed flow and pressure behavior across operating conditions. The final project report documents the following results for the team prototype.

Flow characterization

Flow rates were measured at different rotational speeds, both with the pump alone and with the microfluidic device attached. The pump-only configuration achieved 2.89–17.03 mL/min; the integrated configuration achieved 2.70–10.92 mL/min.

Pressure stability

In three trials at 1,350 RPM, initial hydrostatic pressure was approximately 300–400 Pa and remained relatively stable for about two hours before declining toward zero by six hours. The team identified possible magnetic misalignment and inconsistent impeller rotation as limitations to address in future iterations.

Results summarized from the team's Medtronic BMES Student Design Competition submission. Pressure was estimated from measured water-column height using P = ρgh.

05 / Reflection

Engineering through iteration.

This project strengthened my ability to take a mechanical design from CAD through fabrication, assembly, testing, and repeated iteration. When a prototype did not perform as expected, I learned to use experimental results to identify potential mechanical issues, make targeted design changes, and retest rather than treating the first design as final. It reinforced my interest in hands-on R&D, where mechanical design, fluid behavior, and cross-functional problem-solving come together to improve a physical product.
