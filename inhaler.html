Discreet Inhaler.

A compact inhaler concept developed to make asthma medication use more discreet while exploring pressure-triggered actuation and real-time feedback.

01 / Project Overview

A more discreet handheld format.

Our six-person KGI team started with two challenges: the social discomfort some people experience when using an inhaler in public, and the difficulty of knowing whether an inhalation was performed correctly. We explored a compact, one-handed design with a less medical-looking exterior, pressure sensing, and feedback through an on-device display.

The work included concept sketches, system architecture, component selection, assembly planning, and test protocols. This was an engineering demonstration concept, not a clinically validated drug-delivery device.

02 / Design & Integration

Bringing hardware and interaction together.

Physical design

Considered how the enclosure could accommodate the device's electronics, actuation components, and user-facing features.

Electronic feedback

Mapped a pressure sensor, microcontroller, and solenoid actuator into a breath-triggered system concept.

User interface

Planned OLED status and dose-count feedback, along with visual and tactile indicators in the system architecture.

03 / Prototyping

Making the concept tangible.

The system architecture connected a pressure sensor to a microcontroller, which would interpret inhalation input and control a solenoid valve. An OLED screen and other feedback components were intended to communicate device status. The design also accounted for an MDI cartridge, mouthpiece, rechargeable battery, and 3D-printed housing.

Project documentation

The team produced concept sketches, a functional system diagram, a component bill of materials, assembly instructions, and a design-for-manufacture-and-assembly review. Original prototype photographs and CAD imagery can be added when available.

04 / Engineering Decisions

Designing for assembly and verification.

Beyond the device concept, the team examined how the electronics and housing would be assembled. The DFMA review identified hand-soldered wiring, component alignment, and separate indicator installation as opportunities to simplify the design. Proposed future improvements included an integrated PCB and self-locating housing features.

We also defined measurable targets for cartridge compatibility, dose consistency, portability, battery life, and durability, along with corresponding test protocols. These were planned acceptance criteria, not verified performance results.

05 / Reflection

Designing for the whole device.

What made this project interesting was the combination of physical form, sensing, actuation, and on-device feedback. It reinforced my interest in product development that connects engineering decisions with how someone actually experiences a device.
